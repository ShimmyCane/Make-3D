# Lessons: building a 3-D scene (diorama, animation, game set)

For Jimmy's projects and for any scene built from primitives. Written 2026-11-18 from a research pass and from the Make 3D x Construct sample. Each rule says how strong its source is.

## 1. Plan three planes, and prioritise each

- **Foreground** (nearest the viewer): the most detail and the sharpest edges. Few objects, but each one reads (a horse, a label, a bundle). Put the strongest light and contrast here. (Rule of thumb; several art sources.)
- **Midground** (where the story happens): the main structures and the action. Give it the most *useful* detail, the focal figures and the key props. (Matte-painting guides; the consistent advice in the sources.)
- **Background** (far): less detail, lower contrast, a softer colour. Its job is scale and place (a bluff, a sky). Faint, low-contrast hills make a space feel large. (Diorama and matte-painting sources.)
- **Detail budget:** ask "what does the viewer look at first?" and give that area the detail. Do not spread detail evenly.

## 2. Depth cues that work on a flat screen

- **Overlapping silhouettes.** Let one plane overlap the next. (Several art sources; the strongest single cue.)
- **Value and atmosphere.** Foreground darker and warmer; background lighter, cooler and lower contrast. (Matte painting, widely repeated.)
- **Scale.** A known object (a person, a horse, a lodge) gives the others a size. Check each against a real size first. A 5 ft horse at 1 in = 2 ft is 2.5 in. A 16 ft lodge is 8 in. Our first sample had horses about 11 ft tall; the viewer read them as bison.

## 3. Make each object read as what it is

- **Build from parts.** A single block is not a horse, a lodge or a person. Give each its parts: a horse has a barrel, neck, head, ears, tail and legs; a lodge has a cover, poles and a door.
- **Proportions first.** Get the big shapes right (a horse's barrel and legs) before any small detail. Check against a reference, not a memory.
- **Pose and feet.** A figure needs a torso, arms, legs and feet on the ground, and a pose that makes sense (weight on one leg, arms in motion). A figure standing with both feet flat reads as stiff.
- **Silhouette test.** Fill the whole scene black. Each object should still be recognisable. (Common in animation and game-art practice.)

## 4. Lighting

- One low key light from one side, with soft shadows, gives form. A warm low sun reads as late afternoon. (Diorama builders: overhead light with a dimmer gives useful shadows; art sources agree on a single key.)
- A sky light (a soft fill from above) stops the shadows going black.
- Build the backdrop first and work forward. (Diorama practice.)

## 5. Composition

- Keep the main landmark and the focal figures' poses clear. Simplify the rest. (Diorama guidance.)
- Place figures at slight angles, not in a line against the backdrop. (Diorama guidance.)
- Use the lines of the scene (a travois line, a horse row) to lead the eye to the focal area.

## 6. Process

- **Dry-fit and look from several angles** (top, front, side, oblique) before calling a change done. A flat view hides gaps.
- **Close-up check on every object type** you add.
- **Add objects only for a reason** the scene needs. Density means more detail in the objects that matter first, then more objects in the planes that need them.
- **Keep a list of what is placeholder** (form only, no detail claimed) and phase it out in order.
